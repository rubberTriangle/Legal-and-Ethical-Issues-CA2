What Did You Actually Agree To?

Right at the start, this site asked you to make a cookie choice. Here's what you actually picked, what those categories can mean on a real website, and a few questions worth sitting with before you move on.

Your Cookie Choices

What These Choices Can Mean

This site only keeps a small simulated counter and shows a mock advert elsewhere on the site - it isn't really building a profile of you or sharing anything with a third party. But the categories themselves are real, and on an actual commercial website, accepting them can mean something like this:

Analytics

Depending on the tools a site uses, analytics cookies can be used to record things such as:

  • Which pages you visited
  • How much time you spent on each page
  • Information about your device and browser
  • Your approximate location, depending on the technology used

Advertising / Tracking

Depending on the tools a site uses, advertising and tracking cookies can be used to record things such as:

  • Your general browsing behaviour
  • Interests inferred from your activity
  • Your interactions with adverts
  • Potential tracking across multiple, otherwise unrelated websites

The point of listing this out isn't to suggest that every website does all of it, or that this student site does any of it for real - it's to make the categories on a typical cookie banner feel a bit less abstract than "Analytics" and "Advertising" usually sound.

You clicked a button. But did you really understand everything you were agreeing to?

Necessary cookies are usually easy to picture. Analytics and advertising cookies are more abstract - it's one thing to read the word "tracking," and another to actually picture pages visited, time spent, and inferred interests as one connected profile.

If it did, that's not unusual - it's the whole reason banners are designed the way they are. The more interesting question isn't whether convenience mattered, but how much it should.

Personal data is genuinely valuable to advertisers, which is part of why it's collected so widely. Whether knowing a rough "price" for your own data would change your choices is a fair thing to sit with.

Regulators increasingly say yes - the ePrivacy and GDPR rules covered on the Law & Sources page are partly aimed at exactly this. Whether cookie banners actually achieve it in practice is a separate question.

It's a choice in the technical sense - nobody is forcing your hand. Whether it's a fully informed one, given how these banners are usually designed, is close to the heart of Dilemma 1 on the Ethical Dilemmas page.
