04Law & Sources

Law & Real-World Context

Ethics and law overlap, but they aren't the same thing. Law sets a minimum standard that everyone has to follow, backed by real enforcement, while ethics can ask harder questions than any law currently answers. This page covers the main rules that apply to cookies and personal data in Ireland and the EU, in plain English, followed by the real sources behind every claim made on this site.

GDPR — The General Data Protection Regulation

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is the main EU law on personal data. It has applied since 25 May 2018 and covers any organisation that processes the personal data of people in the EU, even if the organisation itself is based somewhere else. GDPR sets out several core principles that are meant to apply to almost all data processing:

  • Lawfulness, fairness and transparency - processing has to have a valid legal basis and be clearly explained to the person concerned.
  • Purpose limitation - data collected for one stated purpose generally shouldn't be reused for an unrelated purpose without a proper basis.
  • Data minimisation - only the data that's actually needed for the purpose should be collected.
  • Accuracy - personal data should be correct and kept up to date.
  • Storage limitation - data shouldn't be kept for longer than necessary.
  • Integrity and confidentiality - data has to be kept reasonably secure.
  • Accountability - organisations must be able to demonstrate they're actually following these principles, not just state that they do.

Cookies and the ePrivacy Rules

Cookies are actually governed first by a separate piece of law: the EU's ePrivacy Directive, implemented in Ireland through the ePrivacy Regulations (S.I. No. 336 of 2011). The general rule is that non-essential cookies - including analytics and advertising cookies - need the user's consent before they're set, unless the cookie is strictly necessary to provide a service the user has actually asked for.

Because GDPR and the ePrivacy rules are read together, the standard for what counts as valid consent comes from GDPR itself: consent has to be freely given, specific, informed, and given through a clear, affirmative action. In practice, that generally rules out relying on continued browsing as "implied" consent, and rules out pre-ticked boxes for anything non-essential. It's also expected to be as easy to withdraw consent as it was to give it.

Data Protection Commission (DPC) — Ireland's Regulator

The Data Protection Commission is Ireland's independent supervisory authority. It's responsible for enforcing GDPR in Ireland, and it also enforces the ePrivacy Regulations that specifically cover cookies. Because many large technology companies have their EU headquarters in Ireland, the DPC's decisions often end up affecting users right across the EU, not only in Ireland. Among other things, the DPC investigates complaints, carries out its own audits and inquiries (including a review of cookie practices on Irish websites), can order organisations to change how they process data, and can impose fines for serious breaches.

This isn't only theoretical. In 2022, the Irish Council for Civil Liberties (ICCL) - an Irish human rights organisation - published research estimating the scale of "real-time bidding," the automated ad-auction system covered on the Cookies & Data page, and subsequently brought a case to the Irish High Court over the DPC's handling of its complaint about the practice. Whatever the eventual outcome, it's a real, ongoing example of how the rules above get tested in practice, not just written down.

Key Rights Under GDPR

Two ideas sit underneath most of this: the right to privacy, which is protected as a fundamental right under the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, and the requirement for consent to be genuinely informed and freely given rather than assumed. GDPR then gives individuals a set of specific rights they can use, including:

  • The right of access - you can ask an organisation what personal data it holds about you and how it's being used.
  • The right to erasure (sometimes called the "right to be forgotten") - in certain circumstances, you can ask an organisation to delete your personal data.

These rights aren't unlimited - there are legitimate exceptions, for example where data has to be kept for legal reasons - but they exist specifically to stop data collection from being a one-way process.

Law Isn't the Whole Answer

It's entirely possible for a website to be technically compliant with GDPR and the ePrivacy rules while still using a cookie banner that nudges almost everyone toward "Accept All." Compliance is a legal floor, not a guarantee of good design or genuine fairness - which is exactly why the ethical questions on the previous page don't disappear just because the law has been followed.
