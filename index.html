Is Your DataThat Important?

In a world where privacy is becoming harder to protect, are we giving our data away for free?

This site was built for the Legal and Ethical Issues module. It looks at one small but everyday question: what actually happens when you click "Accept" on a cookie banner, and whether most of us really understand what we've just agreed to.

How is this estimated?

This number isn't measured from this site or from you - it's a real published estimate, spread out over time. The Irish Council for Civil Liberties found that "real-time bidding" advertising broadcasts personal data an estimated 178 trillion times a year across the US and Europe combined, which works out to roughly 5.6 million times every second. This counter just multiplies that rate by the seconds you've spent on this page, to make an abstract number feel a bit more concrete. See Law & Sources for the original report.

What Counts as Personal Data?

Personal data is any information that relates to you as an identifiable person. That covers the obvious things, like your name, email address or phone number, but also things people think about less: your IP address, your location history, the device you're browsing on, and even patterns in how you use a website. Under EU law, the definition is deliberately broad, because almost anything can identify someone once it's combined with enough other information.

Why Do Companies Want It?

Data is useful. It helps companies improve their products, understand what users actually do (rather than what they say they do), and decide what to build next. It also has a more direct commercial use: targeted advertising. A lot of "free" websites and apps are only free because advertisers pay to reach specific groups of people, and that targeting depends on data about who you are and what you're interested in.

How Do Websites Collect It?

Some data collection is obvious - you type your email into a sign-up form, or your address into a delivery page. A lot of it is much less visible. Cookies and similar tracking technologies quietly note which pages you visit and for how long. Apps ask for permissions to your camera, contacts or location. Server logs record your IP address and browser automatically. And many pages load code from third parties, such as advertising networks or analytics tools, which can collect data of their own the moment the page loads - often before you've clicked anything at all.

Why Cookies Matter

Cookies are one of the oldest tracking tools on the web, and they're also the reason almost every website now greets you with a consent banner before you've even had a chance to read the headline. They're small, mostly invisible, and often not well understood - which is exactly why this project uses them as the main example throughout.

Why We Accept Without Reading

Privacy policies are long, written in dense legal language, and appear at the exact moment you're trying to do something else - read an article, buy a ticket, watch a video. Faced with a wall of text and a big "Accept All" button, most people take the fast option. That's not necessarily because they don't care about privacy; it's often because the cost of reading everything, every time, feels higher than the cost of just clicking through.

Convenience vs Privacy

This trade-off runs through almost everything on this site. Cookies that remember your login or your shopping basket are genuinely convenient. Cookies that quietly build a profile of your interests for advertisers are a lot less obviously in your interest. The Cookies & Data page looks at this balance in more detail.

Are we making a conscious choice when we give away our data, or are we simply clicking "Accept" because it's easier?

The rest of this site works through these questions from three angles: how cookies and tracking actually work, the ethical dilemmas they raise, and what the law - specifically GDPR and Irish/EU data protection rules - actually says about it.
