02Cookies & Data

Cookies & Personal Data

Cookies are small and mostly invisible, but they're one of the main ways websites collect data about you. Here's what they actually are, the main types, and why they matter more than their size suggests.

What Is a Cookie?

A cookie is a small text file that a website asks your browser to store. It usually contains a small piece of information, such as a unique ID, a language preference, or whether you're logged in. Every time you load a page from that site again, your browser sends the cookie back, which is how the site "remembers" you between requests.

First-Party vs Third-Party Cookies

A first-party cookie is set by the website you're actually visiting, for its own purposes - keeping you logged in, for example. A third-party cookie is set by a different domain, usually one embedded in the page you're on, such as an advertising network, a social media "like" button, or an analytics script. Third-party cookies are what make it possible for a single advertiser to recognise you across many unrelated websites.

Session vs Persistent Cookies

A session cookie is temporary and disappears once you close your browser. A persistent cookie is set to last for a fixed period - anywhere from a few days to a few years - so the site (or a third party) can recognise you the next time you visit.

Analytics Cookies

Analytics cookies are typically used to understand how people use a site: which pages get visited, how long people stay, where they came from, and what device or browser they're using. On their own, this is usually aggregated and reasonably low-risk, though it's still information collected about your specific visit.

Advertising / Tracking Cookies

Advertising and tracking cookies go further. Instead of just measuring a single site, they're often designed to follow the same device across many different websites, building up a picture of your interests over time so that ads can be targeted more precisely.

Why Websites Use Them

Some cookies exist purely so a website works properly - without them, you'd get logged out every time you clicked a link. Others exist for business reasons: analytics cookies help a company understand and improve its product, and advertising cookies are often what makes a "free" service financially viable in the first place.

A Familiar Example

You visit an online shop looking for headphones. You don't buy anything, and you move on. Later that day, on a completely different website - maybe a news site or social media - you see an advert for the exact same headphones. How did that happen?

The likely explanation is a third-party tracking cookie. The shop's page (or an ad network embedded on it) set a cookie linked to your browser. When you later visited a different site that uses the same ad network, that network recognised the cookie, matched it to your earlier browsing, and served an advert based on it. Nothing about this requires your name or email address - a cookie ID linked to a browsing pattern is often enough.

Where Does Your Data Actually Go?

The headphones example makes it sound like just one shop and one ad network are involved. In reality, most online display advertising is sold through an automated auction called real-time bidding (RTB), which runs in well under a second every time a page with ads loads. A "bid request" containing information about you - the page you're on, your rough location, your device, and sometimes much more - is broadcast to a long list of companies at once, all bidding for the chance to show you an ad. Even the companies that don't win the auction still receive the data in that request.

This is where "sold to hundreds of other companies" stops being a figure of speech. Research published by the Irish Council for Civil Liberties (ICCL) in 2022 found that Google alone allows up to 4,698 companies to receive real-time bidding data about people in the US, and estimated that broadcasts like this happen something like 178 trillion times a year across the US and Europe combined. Once that data has gone out, there's generally no reliable way to control what any of those companies do with it afterwards.

None of this means every website works this way, or that accepting cookies on this particular site does anything like it - this is a student project with no real ad auctions running. But it's a real, documented example of what "advertising cookies" can plug into at scale, and it's part of why regulators - including Ireland's own DPC - have been asked to look closely at the practice. See the Law & Sources page for the full reference and the ongoing legal case.

Convenience vs Privacy

Cookies aren't automatically good or bad - it depends on what they're doing and whether you understand and agreed to it. The same underlying technology can make a site more useful or less private, sometimes at the same time.

Convenience

  • Remembering login sessions
  • Remembering language or display preferences
  • Keeping items in a shopping cart
  • Personalised content and recommendations

Privacy concerns

  • Tracking behaviour across sites
  • Building profiles of individual users
  • Targeted advertising based on inferred interests
  • Sharing data with third parties
  • Users not fully understanding what they agreed to
